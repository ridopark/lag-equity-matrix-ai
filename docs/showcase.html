Why a graph database

Same question, both retrievers

Each candidate asks: who supplies this company? The graph answers with named counterparties and the revenue share they disclosed. The vector index answers with the nearest articles in embedding space to the query the node actually issued ("news relevant to a up move in <symbol>"). What this node used to ask

Building this page found a real bug in the pipeline it was demonstrating. For two phases vector_retriever asked a question with almost no content in it, and every assessment the system ever made retrieved market-wrap noise as its news context. Both queries below are real: the left column is what the node asked in production until today.

The score went down, and that is the finding. Cosine similarity measures proximity to the query, not usefulness. A vague query sits near the centre of the corpus, so it scores well against almost everything — the high number was the symptom. The specific query lands further from that centroid and closer to the few articles that matter. Picking the query by score would have selected the worse retriever: a third phrasing we tested scored highest of all, at 0.700, and returned generic stock chatter.

It is uneven, and that is kept rather than tuned away. The same query retrieves more for Apple and Tesla than for Broadcom, because Broadcom has less retail-facing coverage. Tuning the wording until it flattered these three candidates would be exactly the cherry-picking the rest of this page argues against.

Multi-hop

Two hops, with every edge on the path constrained

The supply graph is only one hop deep — see the note below. Depth lives in the co-mention graph, where an edge means two companies were named together in enough articles to clear a PMI floor. Each path here required every edge to clear PMI ≥ 2.0 and to have existed by the as-of date. Paths through ETF tickers are excluded outright: an index fund is co-mentioned with every constituent, so it is a hub that makes any two holdings look two hops apart. "AVGO and TSM connect via SOXX" is an artifact of the index, not a relationship — 1,022 such tickers are filtered from the path.

Why this is not a dataframe query. The constraint is per-path, not per-row: a two-hop path survives only if both of its edges independently clear the weight floor and the date. Expressing that in a dataframe means materialising the transitive closure first and filtering after — which is the work the graph database is doing here in single-digit milliseconds.

And why the supply graph cannot show this. Before the audit it appeared to have two-hop depth. Every one of those paths turned out to run through a competitor list, a divestiture, or a "Baker Hughes rig count" statistic read as a customer relationship. Removing them removed the depth. Of 514 companies, none retains a sound two-hop supply chain — a supplier and its supplier must both name a customer, and that almost never happens. The honest demonstration is on the edge type that has real depth, labelled as such.

Provenance

Every edge traces to a sentence in a filing

The graph is not inferred by a model. Each edge exists because a company wrote its customer's name in a 10-K, and the sentence that created it is carried through to here.

Point in time

The traversal cannot see a filing that had not happened

Every path is guarded per-edge, not per-query. A supplier joins the graph on the date its filing was made and not a day earlier, so a backtest cannot borrow tomorrow's disclosure.


  
Both retrievers, not just the graph. The semantic search is bounded by
    the same date: FILTER … AND a.date < @as_of, strictly before. That was not
    true until recently — the vector adapter shipped with no date predicate at all, and a
    recorded run retrieved an article published 24 days after its as-of. The suite
    passed over it; it was caught by a reviewer reading the retrieved rows on this page. Every
    other guard in this system is enforced by construction; that one was enforced by a comment,
    which is exactly why it failed. The counts below are recomputed each capture.

  
ALL is a quantifier, not a direction. It requires
    every edge along a path to satisfy the predicate; checking only the edge nearest the
    candidate would let a later filing leak in behind an earlier one, which is what one of the
    adapter's tests exists to catch. Do not confuse it with the ANY in the two-hop
    query above — that word chooses edge direction, and the quantified predicates there
    are still ALL.

  
The three properties do not co-occur in this corpus, and that is worth
    saying. The supply edge is directed and date-guarded, but only one hop deep after the
    audit. The co-mention edge reaches two hops and is date-guarded, but it is undirected. A
    traversal that is directed and point-in-time and multi-hop all at once is
    not something this data supports: each property is demonstrated here, the conjunction is
    not.

And the predictive claim came back null

  
This pipeline retrieves and reasons over real structure, and that machinery
    works. Whether the structure predicts returns is a separate question, pre-registered
    and tested: pooled across every supply chain, the one-session lead-lag slope is
    b = −0.0015 against a declared economic threshold of 0.02, with
    I² = 44%. That is a null, and it was re-run on the audited graph rather than inherited
    from the contaminated one. Two other edge types — price correlation and news co-mention —
    returned nulls too. Nothing here is a trading signal.
