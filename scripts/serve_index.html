Which companies would feel it first?

Start from what actually moved today — then ask the filings whether a real relationship explains it.

Why that order matters. The obvious way round is to take a company, look up whoever it correlates with, and check whether they moved. That cannot tell you anything: neighbours picked because they move together will be found to move together — it is the definition, checked against itself. So this starts at the other end. It sweeps every company's own price history for the ones that just moved, then uses the graph to ask whether a genuine, dated, directed relationship exists among them: a company that named the mover as a customer in an SEC filing, filed before the date in question. The graph is used to verify a relationship, not to pick one.

Press Run and it happens in front of you — no recording, no replay. Everything with a number attached was measured during the run you just triggered. The older corroboration-first ordering is still in the dropdown, where a company arrives from an outside alert feed and the graph looks up its neighbours to check the story; it is labelled as such.

What is "limit"? How many companies this run should assess. The feed holds more than that; the limit just takes the first few so a run finishes in a couple of seconds instead of grinding through everything. It is the knob that controls how much parallel work happens — the pipeline starts one independent branch per company, so a limit of 3 means three branches running at once, and 200 means 200. Raising it does not change any answer; it changes how much work you are watching. (The diagram draws the first few branches in full and counts the rest, since 200 identical branches side by side would be unreadable. The timing chart below it still measures every one.)

A large limit looks frozen, briefly. The graph finishes a whole stage for every company before it reports anything, so with 50 companies nothing appears on screen for the first ten seconds or so, and then it all arrives at once. The counter next to the Run button keeps ticking so you can tell it is working rather than stuck.

Which data? Synthetic means invented companies with invented prices — tickers like SYNA and SYNB that do not exist. They ship with the source code, so anyone can clone the repository and press Run without needing credentials, a market-data subscription, or anything private. Nothing you see under that option is a real quote, a real company, or a real trade.

Real market data means genuine share prices and the genuine feed of options alerts this project was built to evaluate. Both are licensed vendor data and a private signal feed, so neither is in the repository — the option only appears when the server is started with --allow-real on a machine that already holds those files locally. Nothing is uploaded anywhere; "this machine only" is literal.

Scan-first starts from the opposite end. The other two sources hand this page a company that an outside alert already flagged, and the graph then looks up its neighbours to check the story. Scan-first never reads that alert feed at all — it looks at every company's own price history, finds the ones that just moved, and only then asks the graph whether a real supplier of that mover exists on paper: a company that named it as a customer in an SEC filing, filed before the date you pick. That ordering is the whole point. Picking neighbours because they move together and then observing that they move together is not evidence of anything — it is the definition, checked against itself. Scan-first uses the graph the way evidence is normally used: to verify a relationship after the fact, not to choose one in the first place. It needs both a machine with real price history and a live connection to the supply-chain graph, so it only appears in the list above when both are available.

The run

Execution graph

Scan-first changes what comes before this diagram, not what is inside it. Steps 1 and 2 at the top ran before the graph was entered: the market was swept for companies that moved, and the graph was traversed to find who supplies them. Only then does the pipeline below run, on the companies that search produced. In the other two sources those first two steps do not exist — a company arrives from an alert feed and the pipeline starts at START.

In plain terms: the work splits into one lane per company. Each lane does two lookups — one in the graph, one in the news — then all the lanes merge back together before any verdict is written. A box lights up only once that step has actually finished, so what you see is the run, not a diagram of it.

The dashed line is a route the code offers but this run didn't take: if a company had no neighbours at all, its lane would stop there instead of continuing. (In LangGraph terms: Send fan-out per candidate, parallel retrieval rejoining at context_fusion, and a conditional edge to END.)

What each step does

find related companies graph_retriever
Looks up the companies connected to this one — suppliers that named it as a customer in an annual report, plus companies whose share price has historically moved with it.
see how they moved leader_state
Checks whether any of those related companies actually moved by an unusual amount in the days before. On most days, most of them did not — which is why so many runs come back "neutral".
search the news vector_retriever
Searches 47,640 real headlines for the ones closest in meaning to this company's situation. Runs at the same time as the step above, not after it.
weigh the evidence context_fusion
Merges the two lanes back together and decides how much each moving company counts for. Companies that always move together are discounted, so one sector-wide wobble is not counted five times as five independent signals. Only the price movements are counted here. The news is attached to the result and shown to you, but carries a weight of exactly zero — see the note under the verdicts.
reach a verdict assessor
Turns that weighted evidence into one of three answers: corroborated, contradicted, or neutral.
pause if contradicted review
If any verdict came back contradicted, the run stops here and waits for confirmation before anything is published. This is the step that makes the whole pipeline resumable — it can halt mid-run and pick up later from a saved snapshot.
write the results publisher
Writes the finished assessments out. Nothing reaches this step until the pause above has been cleared.
stop early END
The dashed route. If a company turns out to have no related companies at all, there is nothing to assess, so that branch stops instead of continuing. No branch took it this run.

What it concluded

What it decided — and the number it refuses to give

What a verdict means: the pipeline looks at how each company's neighbours moved, and asks whether that supports the idea that this company is about to move the same way. Three outcomes: corroborated (the neighbours agree), contradicted (they point the other way), or neutral (not enough moved to say).

What actually decides it — and on scan runs it is mostly one new thing. Two inputs move the verdict. The first is how price-correlated neighbours moved, unchanged, and measured to be worth nothing on its own: −4.6 bp, z = −0.72. The second exists only when the scan found the company itself — then whether the shocked company that surfaced it has already been echoed counts too. On the most recent full scan (19 candidates) that second input changed the verdict for 11 of them, because 11 had no correlated-neighbour evidence at all and were previously dropped as unassessable. Where both inputs existed, all 8 agreed — which is less reassuring than it sounds, since both read the candidate's own move, and how far they are genuinely independent is an open question, not a settled one.

Already moved is not the same as wrong. A candidate that has caught up with the company that surfaced it is marked already moved and sorted toward the bottom, contributing no evidence in either direction — the opportunity is spent, not refuted, and collapsing those two into one verdict would bury good names among disproved ones. Worth knowing: on that same scan, no candidate landed in that bucket. The bar is "moved at least as far as a company that just moved two-plus standard deviations", which almost nothing clears in three sessions. So the state label currently tells you nothing the verdict does not — every "open" name is corroborated and every "moved against" name is contradicted, exactly. The one genuinely new number on a card is room, and only its size distinguishes the open names from one another: on that scan it ran from 0.99 down to 0.39.

Room is a place to look, not a forecast. It is a comparison between candidates in each company's own volatility units, and nothing more. It is deliberately not multiplied by any transfer coefficient, because we measured that coefficient for this exact customer → supplier relationship and it came back b = −0.0015 against a pre-registered 0.02 threshold — a null, on the wrong side of zero. So no expected move, no target, no figure in bp or per cent appears on any card. Assuming some fraction of the leader's move must reappear in the candidate would be asserting the one number this project went looking for and did not find.

What still does not vote. The news is fetched, scored, attached to the result and displayed, and then given a weight of 0.0 — it is context for a human reading the output. The supply-chain links do not vote either: those run customer → supplier, so a supplier moving says nothing about its customer, and the code filters them out of the count on purpose. Both are retrieved because a person judging the verdict should see them; neither is allowed to change it. That is a deliberate choice, not an oversight — nothing here has been shown to predict returns, so weighting it would be inventing confidence.

What went wrong, and what didn't work

Two things worth saying plainly, because a demo that only shows its good side is not worth much.

First: a third of the connections were wrong. The extractor originally found 1,211 "this company is our customer" relationships. Re-reading each one against the single sentence that actually names the other company left 818. The rest were companies listing their competitors, announcing acquisitions, or naming a supplier and getting read backwards. Two were South Dakota v. Wayfair — a Supreme Court sales-tax case that the extractor read as a business relationship.

Second: none of it predicts anything. The obvious hope is that if a big customer moves today, its suppliers move tomorrow. We tested that, having written down in advance what would count as a real effect. The answer: a 1% move in the customer is followed by roughly −0.15 basis points on its suppliers the next day. We had declared beforehand that we would need at least 2 basis points to call it tradeable — so the result is more than ten times too small, and pointing the wrong way. It was not consistent year to year either. Two other kinds of link were tested and also came back empty. Nothing here is a trading signal. (b = −0.0015 against a pre-registered threshold of 0.02, I² = 44%, re-run on the cleaned graph rather than inherited from the dirty one.)

And one honest limitation about the graph itself. A supply link has a direction (the supplier names its customer, not the other way round) and a date (we know when it was disclosed) — but after the cleanup above, it is only ever one step deep. Going two steps needs a supplier and its own supplier to both name a customer publicly, which almost never happens. The news link does reach two steps and is dated, but it has no direction: "mentioned together" does not tell you who leads whom. So each of those three properties is real here. All three at once is not something this data can show, and this page does not pretend otherwise.
