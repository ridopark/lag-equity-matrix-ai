What moved today, and what moves with it

Pick a day. See which companies moved unusually for them. Click one, and see which other companies have actually moved with it — measured over a decade, with a confidence interval on every number.

The honest version of what this is. This project set out to find companies that move after a leader does — a tradeable lag. It does not exist in this data. We tested it properly: every pair in the universe, 2.47 million of them, at five different delays, over ten years, choosing the pairs in one window and checking them in a completely separate one. A relationship worth 0.21 when you pick it is worth 0.03 when you test it. Chains of relationships fare no better. Three pre-registered tests, all null.

What is there instead, and it is not small. The same-day version of the same measurement survives that test almost intact: 0.59. Companies that have moved together reliably keep moving together, and that holds up across four years of data the measurement never saw. So this page shows you that, and stops there. It will tell you Palo Alto Networks moves with CrowdStrike and Fortinet; it will not tell you which one moves first, because we looked and nothing does.

Isn't that circular? A fair challenge, and it is why this project spent months on the supply chain instead. Picking neighbours because they correlate and then citing their correlation as evidence for a forecast is circular. But "these two have moved together, and here is how precisely that is measured" is a description, not a forecast — and it is falsifiable: measure it on 2016–2022, check it on 2022–2026, see whether it holds. It does. That is the difference between the number below and the one this project could not find.

Pick a day, pick something that moved, see what moves with it

Two steps, both live. One: sweep every company's own price history for the day you choose and keep the ones whose last three sessions were unusually large for them. Two: click one, and see the companies whose prices have actually moved with it over the past year — measured, with a confidence interval on every number.

What the number is, and is not. It is a correlation: how reliably these two have moved together, same day. It is not a forecast. We tested whether one company's move anticipates another's — every pair in the universe, five lags, ten years, out of sample — and it does not: the relationship that looks like 0.21 in one window is worth 0.03 in the next. Same-day co-movement is the opposite: it holds up at 0.59. So the bars below say "these move together", never "this one moves next".

"limit" is how many companies to assess — one independent branch each, so 3 means three running at once. It changes how much work you watch, never an answer. A large limit looks frozen briefly: the graph finishes a whole stage for every company before reporting anything, so with 50 nothing appears for ten seconds and then it all arrives at once. The counter keeps ticking so you can tell it is working.

Which data? Synthetic is invented companies with invented prices — SYNA, SYNB — shipped with the source so anyone can clone the repository and press Run with no credentials. Nothing under that option is a real quote.

Real market data is genuine share prices and the genuine options-alert feed this project was built to evaluate. Both are licensed vendor data and a private feed, so neither is in the repository — the option appears only when the server is started with --allow-real on a machine that already holds those files. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.

Scan-first sweeps every company's price history for the ones that just moved, then asks the supply graph whether a real supplier of that mover exists on paper — a company that named it as a customer in an SEC filing, dated before the day in question. It is the strictest thing here, and also the narrowest: the graph holds 76 customers, so on a typical day only a handful of movers have any disclosed supplier at all. Co-movement above covers far more — 36 of 43 movers on 2026-09-04, against 2 by filings. The filing is better evidence about any single pair; there are just very few pairs.

Four steps. The first two decide which companies are worth looking at; the last two decide what to think about each one. Everything below is drawn from the run itself — a box lights up only once that step has actually finished.

  1. Find out what moved. Sweep every company in the price file and keep the ones that moved by an unusual amount in the last three sessions. Nothing about the graph is consulted yet.
  2. Ask who is connected to them. For each of those movers, walk the supply graph to the companies that named it as a customer in an annual report. Those become the candidates.
  3. Gather what is known about each candidate. One lane per candidate, all running at once. Each lane looks up the company's price-correlated neighbours and checks whether any of them moved, and — at the same time — searches the news for the company itself.
  4. Weigh it and answer. The lanes merge, related companies that always move together are discounted so one sector-wide wobble is not counted five times, and each candidate gets one of three answers.

Steps 1 and 2 only exist in scan mode. With an alert feed as the source, a company arrives already chosen and the run starts at step 3 — the diagram below begins at START with nothing above it.

The dashed line is a route the code offers but this run didn't take: a company with no related companies at all stops there instead of continuing. (In LangGraph terms: Send fan-out per candidate, parallel retrieval rejoining at context_fusion, and a conditional edge to END.)

The same four steps, in detail

The name in grey under each one is the pipeline node, if you want to read the code.

1 · find out what moved MarketScan.shocked_leaders
Sweeps every company in the price file and keeps those whose last three sessions are large relative to their own recent volatility. This is where a run starts: no company is proposed until something has actually happened to it.
2 · ask who is connected MarketScan.candidates
Walks the supply graph from each mover to the companies that named it as a customer in an annual report, using only filings dated before the day being assessed. Each candidate remembers which mover surfaced it.
3a · see how neighbours moved graph_retriever → leader_state
Looks up the companies whose share price has historically moved with this one, then checks whether any of them moved unusually in the days before. On most days most of them did not, which is why so many runs come back "neutral". The lookup has to come first here — you cannot ask how a company's neighbours moved before knowing who they are.
3b · search the news vector_retriever
Searches 47,640 real headlines for the ones closest in meaning to this company's situation. It needs only the company's ticker, so it runs at the same time as 3a rather than after it. (It is still dispatched after the neighbour lookup, for a scheduling reason rather than a real dependency — see D-89.)
4 · weigh it context_fusion
Merges the lanes and decides how much each moving company counts for. Companies that always move together are discounted, so one sector-wide wobble is not counted five times as five independent signals. Only the price movements are counted here. The news is attached and shown to you, but carries a weight of exactly zero — see the note under the verdicts.
4 · answer assessor
Turns that weighted evidence into corroborated, contradicted, or neutral.
pause if contradicted review
If any verdict came back contradicted, the run stops and waits for confirmation before anything is published. This is the step that makes the pipeline resumable — it can halt mid-run and pick up later from a saved snapshot.
write the results publisher
Writes the finished assessments out. Nothing reaches this step until the pause above has been cleared.
stop early END
The dashed route. A company with no related companies at all has nothing to assess, so that branch stops instead of continuing.

What it concluded

What it decided — and the number it refuses to give

What a verdict means: the pipeline looks at how each company's neighbours moved over the same window, and asks whether they moved consistently with this company. Three outcomes: corroborated (the neighbours agree), contradicted (they point the other way), or neutral (not enough moved to say). It is a reading of what happened, in the past tense throughout — the forward-looking version of this question was tested three times and came back null, which is the subject of the last section on this page.

What actually decides it — and it is one thing, in every mode. Of everything retrieved above, the verdict turns on a single input: how price-correlated neighbours moved. Measured on its own, that input is worth nothing — −4.6 bp, z = −0.72. A card from a scan run also carries a one-line description of what the company itself has already done and who surfaced it. Like the news below, it is shown because a person judging the verdict should see it, and it is not allowed to change it.

There used to be a second input here. We measured it and removed it. It scored each scan candidate by how much of its leader's move was still ahead of it — a ratio, sorted, with the least-moved names on top. Two independent measurements killed it: a study over 432 scan dates and 13,063 supplier-events, and a separate rebuild over 30 dates and 209 linked events using different data and different code. Dividing one company's move by another's quietly assumes the first should travel the second's full distance; measured, that pass-through is +0.18 the same day and about zero forward, and it does not grow with the size of the leader's shock in either sample. The ordering it existed to produce was flat against forward returns in both. And the names it called "still has room" earned exactly what the names it called "moved against" earned (+0.084 vs +0.084 in one sample, −0.206 vs −0.206 in the other) — the split carried nothing the correlation vote above did not already have. The co-movement it treated as a supply-chain effect turns out to be the same trailing price correlation that vote already uses.

What replaced it is deliberately inert. A sentence: how far this company has already moved, in its own volatility units, and which shocked company surfaced it. No threshold, no category, no ratio, no ranking — a fact about what already happened, not a claim about what happens next. Removing a number is cheaper than defending one that a measurement says is noise, and the finding is kept on the page rather than quietly deleted with the code.

What still does not vote. The news is fetched, scored, attached to the result and displayed, and then given a weight of 0.0 — it is context for a human reading the output. The supply-chain links do not vote either: those run customer → supplier, so a supplier moving says nothing about its customer, and the code filters them out of the count on purpose. Both are retrieved because a person judging the verdict should see them; neither is allowed to change it. That is a deliberate choice, not an oversight — nothing here has been shown to predict returns, so weighting it would be inventing confidence.

What went wrong, and what didn't work

Two things worth saying plainly, because a demo that only shows its good side is not worth much.

First: a third of the connections were wrong. The extractor originally found 1,211 "this company is our customer" relationships. Re-reading each one against the single sentence that actually names the other company left 818. The rest were companies listing their competitors, announcing acquisitions, or naming a supplier and getting read backwards. Two were South Dakota v. Wayfair — a Supreme Court sales-tax case that the extractor read as a business relationship.

Second: none of it predicts anything. The obvious hope is that if a big customer moves today, its suppliers move tomorrow. We tested that, having written down in advance what would count as a real effect. The answer: a 1% move in the customer is followed by roughly −0.15 basis points on its suppliers the next day. We had declared beforehand that we would need at least 2 basis points to call it tradeable — so the result is more than ten times too small, and pointing the wrong way. It was not consistent year to year either.

Then we stopped testing links and tested everything. Rather than argue about which kind of relationship to look at, the last test took every pair of companies in the universe — 2,472,756 of them — at five different delays, over ten years, picking the strongest pairs in one window and checking them in a separate four-year window the measurement had never seen. Pairs that looked like 0.21 when chosen were worth 0.03 when tested. A shuffled control, which destroys any real timing relationship, scored as well or better at four of the five delays. Chains of relationships added nothing: they beat a coin flip on direction and carried no size at all. That null is the well-powered one — a real effect of the size worth trading could not have been missed at that sample. The same measurement done same-day instead of next-day survives the identical test at 0.59, which is what the top of this page shows you.

That 0.59 was 0.64 earlier today, and the correction is worth showing. A separate test returned an average move of eleven thousand standard deviations — obviously impossible, and chasing it found two faults in the price file. Eleven "returns" above 1000%: bankruptcy emergences, reverse splits and reused tickers, where a company ends and a different one inherits the symbol. And a handful of names whose price never moved for months, so dividing by their volatility divided by almost zero — one produced a "move" of 593 million sigma. Neither is a market event; both are the data being wrong. Removing them lowers the headline from 0.64 to 0.59. The conclusion does not change — 0.59 against 0.03 for the next-day version is the same gap — but the number was wrong while it was on this page, and the honest thing is to say so rather than quietly swap it. Nothing here is a trading signal. (b = −0.0015 against a pre-registered threshold of 0.02, I² = 44%, re-run on the cleaned graph rather than inherited from the dirty one.)

And one honest limitation about the graph itself. A supply link has a direction (the supplier names its customer, not the other way round) and a date (we know when it was disclosed) — but after the cleanup above, it is only ever one step deep. Going two steps needs a supplier and its own supplier to both name a customer publicly, which almost never happens. The news link does reach two steps and is dated, but it has no direction: "mentioned together" does not tell you who leads whom. So each of those three properties is real here. All three at once is not something this data can show, and this page does not pretend otherwise.
